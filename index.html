You can do this

Nobody was born knowing how to use AI. The reps who get good at it are the ones who try one small, safe thing and keep going. AI handles the busywork so you can spend more time on the part only you can do: listening, building trust, and helping a customer decide.

The traffic-light rule

Every use case in this guide gets one of three lights.

Green, safe. Your own writing or public information, in a tool your company approved. Example: tighten an email you wrote.
Amber, guardrails. Touches customer or company data. Allowed only with the listed control and sign-off. Example: summarize a recorded call.
Red, not safe. Breaks privacy, confidentiality, or honesty. Example: pasting a customer contract into a free chatbot.

AI basics in five minutes

No technical background needed.

What it is

An AI assistant is a program you chat with. You type a request, called a prompt, and it writes a reply. It predicts likely words from patterns it learned, so it sounds confident whether or not it is right.

What it is good at

Drafting, rewriting, summarizing, brainstorming, practicing conversations, and organizing messy notes.

What it is bad at

Exact facts and numbers, math, live traffic or news, your private deal details, and knowing when it is wrong.

Four rules to start with

  1. Ask Legal and IT first. They decide which tools and data are allowed.
  2. Do not paste what you would not post publicly unless the tool is approved for that kind of data.
  3. You are the author. Check every fact, name, number, and promise before it leaves your hands.
  4. Start small. Begin with green use cases and move up only with approval.

How to write a better prompt

Give it a role, a task, context, and the format you want.

Weak
Write me an email.
Strong
Act as a sales coach. Rewrite the follow-up email below for a busy operations manager. Keep it under 90 words and end with one clear question.

Four first prompts to try

Replace the bracketed parts. Use only content that is public or your own, in a tool your company approved.

Improve your own email
Act as a friendly sales coach. Here is a cold email I wrote: [paste your draft]. Make it shorter, keep my tone, and give me three subject line options.
Practice objections
Play a skeptical purchasing manager at a mid-size manufacturing company. I will pitch you. Push back with realistic objections about price and timing. After five exchanges, give me feedback on what I did well and what to improve.
Plan a route of stops
I am visiting these businesses tomorrow: [list of business names and street addresses]. Suggest the most logical order to reduce backtracking, group nearby stops, and put the most important meeting at the best time of day. Tell me what to double-check in a mapping app.
Prepare from a public article
Summarize the key points of this public article in five bullets, then suggest three questions I could ask a prospect in this industry: [paste article text].

When the AI states something false in a confident tone, such as a fake quote or statistic.

A company-managed plan with a contract and admin controls. Safer than a personal account for work.

When a vendor uses what you type to improve its AI. Business plans usually say they do not. Always check.

Anything that identifies a person, such as names, emails, phone numbers, or health details.
Shadow IT
Using tools your company has not approved. The biggest AI risk for sales teams.
A note to every seller

AI is not replacing the part of selling you love. It is taking away the part you don't.

Trust, curiosity, and judgment are what close deals, and they are yours. AI is a tool that gives you more time and more room to use them.

It gives time back

Call notes, CRM updates, account research, first drafts, and scheduling add up to hours each week. AI can take a first pass at all of them.

It lowers the blank-page fear

Staring at an empty email is hard. Starting from a draft you can reshape is easy. You stay the author; you just stop starting from zero.

It helps you walk in prepared

Role-play a tough buyer, learn an unfamiliar industry in minutes, and arrive at each call with sharper questions.

Why this is the path forward

  • Buyers are already using it. More buyers research vendors with AI before they ever talk to you. Meeting them there is part of the job.
  • The routine work is shrinking. What stays valuable is judgment, relationships, and creativity, which are the skills sellers are best at.
  • Skills compound. A rep who learns to prompt, check, and refine today gets better every month. The gap between early learners and late ones widens.
  • It rewards good habits. Teams that pair AI speed with human review and clear rules get the benefits without the headlines.

How outreach becomes scalable

Before AI, personal outreach meant a rep could only research and write so many messages a day. With AI doing the first pass, one rep can give more people a message that is actually relevant to them.

1
Research. AI gathers public context on the account and role.
2
Draft. AI writes a first version in your voice with a clear reason to reach out.
3
Review. You check facts, tone, and fit, and cut anything that feels off.
4
Send and learn. Track replies and feed what worked into your next prompts.

Scale quality, not just volume. Messages that are generic, inaccurate, or unwanted hurt your name and your company's, and may break the law. Keep a human in the loop and honor opt-outs.

Three kinds of AI help

Most sales AI falls into one of three groups. Knowing which one you are using tells you how much to check and who needs to approve it. A good path is to start with augmentation, add analysis, and automate last.

Augmentation

AI works with you

You stay in the driver's seat. AI drafts, brainstorms, explains, and coaches, and you decide what to use.

Examples: rewriting an email, role-playing a buyer, preparing call questions, outlining a proposal.

Tools: chat assistants from any of the eight tools in the Compare tab, plus assistants built into email, documents, and meetings.

Main risk: trusting a confident but wrong answer. Your job: review every output.

Start level: easiest, mostly green.

Analysis

AI finds the pattern

AI reads lots of data or text and surfaces what matters, so you can decide where to spend time.

Examples: forecast commentary, lead scoring, call themes, win/loss patterns, churn risk, account prioritization.

Tools: analytics inside your CRM, call analytics tools, spreadsheets and BI tools with AI, and long-document assistants.

Main risk: poor data, bias, and false precision. Your job: sanity-check findings and decide.

Start level: moderate, often amber.

Automation

AI does the task

AI completes steps on its own once set up: moving data, routing leads, sending sequences, transcribing calls.

Examples: logging calls, updating CRM fields, routing inbound leads, scheduling follow-ups, running outreach sequences.

Tools: workflow and sales-engagement platforms, CRM automations, and custom agents built by IT on cloud or in-house models.

Main risk: one mistake repeats at scale. Your job: set limits, audit samples, and keep an off switch.

Start level: highest, needs Legal and IT.

Best practices

Habits that make AI useful and keep you out of trouble.

Ask better

  • Give it a role, a task, context, and a format.
  • Say who the reader is and what you want them to do.
  • Paste a sample of your own writing so it matches your tone.
  • Ask for three options, then choose.
  • Tell it what to avoid, such as jargon or hype.
  • Ask it to ask you questions before it answers.
  • Iterate. The second prompt is usually better than the first.

Check everything

  • Verify every fact, number, name, and date against a source.
  • Ask for sources, then open them.
  • Recalculate math and ROI in a spreadsheet.
  • Read the whole draft aloud before sending.
  • Confirm product, pricing, and compliance claims with the owner.
  • Treat confident tone as a warning sign, not proof.

Protect data

  • Use only company-approved accounts.
  • Share the minimum needed, and swap names for placeholders.
  • Never paste passwords, keys, payment, or health data.
  • Know whether your plan trains on your inputs.
  • Keep customer contracts and unreleased plans out unless approved.
  • Report mistakes quickly. Early reports are cheap.

Earn customer trust

  • Be relevant, not creepy. Reference professional context only.
  • Keep your own voice and real opinions in the message.
  • Disclose AI use where law or contract requires.
  • Never claim to be human if someone sincerely asks.
  • Honor unsubscribe and do-not-contact requests.
  • Never invent references, quotes, or urgency.

Build habits

  • Start with one task and time-box it.
  • Keep a personal library of prompts that worked.
  • Use AI for the first draft, not the last word.
  • Keep a short log of wins and misses.
  • Review automations on a schedule, with a sample audit.
  • Share what you learn with your team.

For managers

  • Publish simple rules using the three traffic lights.
  • Create a shared, approved prompt library.
  • Measure outcomes such as response quality and time saved, not usage alone.
  • Make it safe to admit mistakes.
  • Keep a direct line to Legal and IT.
  • Coach the checking habit as much as the prompting habit.

Three questions before any prompt

Answer these in order. One bad answer is enough to stop or escalate.

1. What data goes in?

  • Public: websites, filings, press releases. Lowest risk.
  • Internal: playbooks, non-public pricing, forecasts. Approved enterprise tool only.
  • Customer or regulated: contracts, personal data, health, payment. Needs written approval from Legal and IT.

2. Who checks the output?

  • A named person reads everything before it reaches a customer.
  • Numbers, quotes, names, and claims get verified against a source.
  • Nothing sends, saves to the CRM, or commits to terms on its own.

3. What if it is wrong?

  • Low: a clumsy draft only you see.
  • Medium: a wrong fact in a customer email.
  • High: a pricing, legal, security, or compliance statement. Needs expert sign-off.

Safe Green

Public or your own non-confidential content, approved tool, you review. Go.

Guardrails Amber

Touches internal or customer data, or affects customers. Allowed only with the control listed on the use case and sign-off from Legal and IT.

Not safe Red

Breaks confidentiality, privacy law, consent, or honesty. Do not do it, even in an approved tool, unless Legal explicitly says otherwise.

Laws and rules that touch sales AI

A plain-language overview, not legal advice. Rules differ by country and state and change often. Ask Legal what applies to you.

Anti-spam and marketing email

Laws such as CAN-SPAM in the US, CASL in Canada, and ePrivacy rules in the EU and UK cover commercial email. Under US CAN-SPAM, the FTC says headers and subject lines must be accurate, messages must include a valid physical address and a clear opt-out, opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days, and each violating email can carry penalties of up to $53,088 (FTC figure). AI-written volume does not change the rules. Sources: [L1]

Privacy and personal data

GDPR, UK GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, and many state laws limit how personal data is collected and used. Putting personal data into an AI tool is processing it, which can require a lawful basis, vendor terms, and transparency.

Call recording

Some places require everyone's consent and others only one party's. AI note-takers and transcription count as recording. Ask Legal about every region you sell in.

Automated calls and texts

In the US, the TCPA restricts autodialed and prerecorded calls and texts, and in February 2024 the FCC ruled that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" voices under the TCPA, so calls generally need prior express consent. Consent rules are strict. Sources: [L3]

AI transparency

Some laws and contracts require telling people when they are talking to an AI or when content is AI-generated, such as Article 50 of the EU AI Act, which requires people to be told they are interacting with an AI system and requires disclosure of deepfakes. The text source lists these duties as applying from 2 August 2026, so confirm the current status with Legal. Sources: [L2]

Confidentiality and contracts

NDAs and customer agreements often limit sharing information with third parties, which can include AI vendors. Check before pasting anything covered.

Regulated industries

Health (HIPAA), financial services (GLBA and securities rules), payments (PCI), and public-sector buyers add stricter requirements. Ask before selling or using AI there.

Copyright and brand

AI output can resemble existing work. Check images, text, and music before using them publicly, and follow your brand and approval rules.

If you slip

Pasted something you should not have? It happens. Fast, honest reporting is what keeps a small mistake small.

1
Stop. Do not paste or send anything else.
2
Do not delete or hide it. Leave the evidence so IT can assess it.
3
Write down the basics. What you shared, which tool and account, and when.
4
Tell your manager, IT, and Legal right away. Early reports have the most options.
5
Follow their instructions. That may include deleting data with the vendor or notifying a customer.
6
Learn from it. Share what you would do differently so the team benefits.

Agents and connected tools: extra care

AI agents can read your email, browse the web, and take actions. That is powerful, and it is a new kind of risk.

  • Hidden instructions are real. A web page or email can contain text that tries to steer the AI. This is called prompt injection.
  • Give the least access that works. Start read-only. Do not grant send, delete, or payment rights early.
  • Keep a person approving outside actions. Sending an email or changing a record should wait for a human.
  • Never put secrets in prompts or connected files. Passwords and keys belong in a vault.
  • Know how to turn it off. Have an off switch and a log of what the agent did.
  • Ask IT first. Connecting tools to your accounts needs security review.

Rules that apply to every vendor

  • The plan matters more than the brand. Free and personal accounts often have different data terms than business plans.
  • Use work accounts only. Never move work data through a personal account.
  • Check call recording and transcription consent rules in every region you sell in.
  • Disclose AI where law or contract requires it, and never deny being an AI when someone sincerely asks.
  • Ask for the vendor's data retention, training, and subprocessor terms in writing.

Weak AI draft

After your edit
"Hi Priya, I saw Northwind is hiring an operations lead. When that role opens, keeping scheduling consistent across plants is often the first thing to slip. Is that on your list this quarter? I can share a one-page example of how another fabricator handled it. Sam"
  • Specific reason to write. A public trigger replaces flattery.
  • No unproven claims. "Hundreds of companies" and "revolutionize" are gone.
  • One clear question. The reader knows what to do next.
  • You verified it. Only claim "another fabricator" if it is true and you can share it.

What to say to customers

Starting points only. Use the wording Legal approves for your company.

"Are you using AI on my data?"

"Fair question. Here is exactly how we handle customer data: [approved statement]. If you need more detail, I will connect you with our security team."

Never: guess or say "no" without confirming.

"Did AI write this email?"

"I used AI to help draft it, and I reviewed and edited every line. Happy to tell you more about how I work."

Never: deny it when someone sincerely asks.

Asking to use an AI note-taker

"I would like to use an AI note-taker so I can focus on you. It records and transcribes this call. Is that okay with everyone?"

Never: start without consent where it is required.

"How accurate is your product's AI?"

"Here is what it does well, here is where a person should review, and here is how we measure it: [approved facts]."

Never: promise accuracy you cannot prove.

A prospect sends a long AI-written message

Answer the real question inside it. Reply short and human, and ask one clarifying question.

Tip: AI polish does not mean low intent.

A customer asks for sensitive advice

"That is a legal question, so I would rather connect you with the right expert than guess."

Never: pass AI answers off as legal, tax, or compliance advice.

Questions reps ask

Will AI replace me?

AI is good at drafts, summaries, and patterns. Buyers still choose people they trust, and trust, judgment, and relationships stay human. Reps who use AI well will likely outperform those who do not, so learning it is the safest career move.

Will I be judged on how much I use it?

That is a question for your manager. Healthy teams measure outcomes, such as quality and time saved, rather than raw usage.

What if the AI is wrong and I did not notice?

It happens to everyone. Follow the checking habits, report fast if something went out, and fix it with the customer. See "If you slip" on the Safety tab.

Can I use my own free account because it is faster?

Not for work data. Personal accounts often have weaker data terms and no company visibility. Ask IT for an approved option.

Do I have to tell customers I use AI?

Sometimes the law or the contract requires it, and you should never deny it when sincerely asked. Ask Legal what applies to your company.

My manager asked me to do something marked red. What now?

Pause and ask Legal or IT before doing it. Raise it calmly and put the question in writing. That protects you and the company.

Is using AI cheating?

No. It is a tool, like a CRM or a calculator. What matters is honesty, accuracy, and using approved tools.

How do I get better at it?

Pick one task a week, keep the prompts that work, compare results, and share what you learn with teammates.
